Bruce Peninsula

Paddling the Bruce

Some places are worth visiting once. Others keep calling you back.

The Bruce Peninsula was one of the first places that showed me just how far a kayak could take me. My first trip began with a photograph of Flowerpot Island and an almost complete lack of understanding of what I was getting myself into. I arrived curious, inexperienced, and determined to see it for myself.

Since then, I've returned again and again — sometimes with friends, sometimes with a partner, and sometimes simply following the coastline on my own. Each trip has revealed something different. Changing weather, changing water conditions, different seasons, and even different directions of travel have made familiar places feel completely new.

I've paddled toward the Grotto from Dunks Bay more than once, explored the coast around Lion's Head, ventured out from Dyers Bay, and returned years later to places I thought I already knew. Some trips were calm and effortless. Others were humbling reminders that the Great Lakes can change their personality very quickly.

Perhaps the most interesting thing I've learned is that returning to a place doesn't necessarily mean repeating an experience. With more time on the water, I became less interested in simply reaching a destination and more interested in everything I had previously paddled past.

These are a few of those journeys.

Shorter adventures, different coastlines, changing conditions, unexpected moments, and some of the places that have made the Bruce Peninsula one of my favourite paddling destinations.

A Coastline Built From Stone

The Bruce Peninsula is not simply beautiful. It is geologically strange, almost impossibly varied, and constantly changing from one stretch of coastline to the next.

The peninsula is shaped by the Niagara Escarpment, a massive geological formation that stretches across Ontario. Along the water, layers of limestone, dolostone and shale create cliffs, shelves and exposed rock that seem to change colour and character every few hundred metres.

Brilliant white limestone gives way to darker grey dolostone and bands of blue-grey shale. Fossils are embedded throughout the ancient rock, quiet reminders that this landscape was formed in a world unimaginably different from the one we paddle through today.

In other places, the cliffs disappear entirely into cobblestone beaches, rock gardens and enormous boulders scattered along the shoreline. Smooth stones polished by thousands of years of water sit beside fractured slabs and towering escarpment walls. Some sections feel carefully arranged, almost like a landscape designed rather than naturally formed.

Between the rocks are alvars, open limestone landscapes where surprisingly little soil supports an unusual collection of plants. Elsewhere, ancient cedars cling to cracks in the cliffs and grow at angles that seem to defy both gravity and common sense.

And then there is the water.

Clear enough to see the bottom from a kayak.

Depending on the depth, weather and sunlight, the water can shift from almost transparent shallows to turquoise, emerald green and deep blue. Beneath the surface are underwater cliffs, rounded stones, hidden shelves, and shipwrecks that have become part of the landscape itself.

The Bruce Peninsula is also a very different paddling environment from the remote Crown Land adventures further north. There is essentially nowhere to simply disappear and legally establish a backcountry campsite. Most of the peninsula is surrounded by parks, private property or heavily visited areas, making these primarily day-trip adventures.

And busy they can be.

In the most popular areas, beaches, marinas, tour boats and sightseeing crowds can make it easy to forget how wild the coastline actually is. But a kayak changes the equation.

Paddle a few kilometres away from the obvious destinations and the crowds begin to disappear. The beaches become empty. The tour boats become occasional silhouettes in the distance. A fishing boat might pass, another adventurous paddler might appear around a corner, and then the coastline belongs to you again.

That is the Bruce Peninsula I keep coming back to.

Three Ways Into the Bruce

There is more than one way to experience this coastline.

Over the years, I've returned to three different launch points, discovering that each route reveals a completely different personality of the landscape.

These adventures are shorter than my multi-day expeditions, but that doesn't make them any less memorable. Some were relaxed day trips. Others involved rougher water, changing weather, unexpected challenges, and moments that reminded me that even familiar water deserves respect.
