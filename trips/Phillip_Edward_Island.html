Phillip Edward Island

2021-2024

Paddling Paradise

Some places impress you the first time you see them. Others slowly become part of you.

My relationship with Phillip Edward Island began with curiosity, a little too much confidence, and a map that I didn't yet fully understand.

My first trip into the Killarney region was supposed to take me toward Phillip Edward Island. Instead, I launched from Killarney Bay, missed the channel I was looking for, and eventually found myself paddling around George Island wondering exactly where I had gone wrong.

It was not the most graceful introduction to the region. But somewhere along that confused route, I caught my first distant glimpse of the La Cloche Mountains rising above the horizon.

I had no idea what I was looking at.

I only knew that I had never seen anything like it.

That first glimpse stayed with me.

I returned to the Killarney region again and again, gradually learning the maze of islands, channels and exposed granite that makes Georgian Bay so different from the other places I had paddled.

Eventually I reached Phillip Edward Island properly. What I found was far beyond the distant landscape I had first imagined. Pink granite stretched beneath windswept pines. Narrow channels opened into enormous expanses of open water. Islands appeared, disappeared and reappeared behind one another, creating a landscape that seemed to have no obvious beginning or end.

Unlike the Bruce Peninsula, where dramatic destinations can often be reached in a single day, this part of Georgian Bay rewards time. The farther you travel, the more the landscape reveals itself. A channel leads to another island. An island leads to another bay. A promising campsite becomes the starting point for another day's exploration.

I began returning not simply because I wanted to see Phillip Edward Island, but because I wanted to understand it.

Some trips were carefully planned. Others were shaped by wind, waves, mistakes, unexpected discoveries and the simple realization that the place I had intended to reach was not necessarily where I needed to go.

Somewhere along the way, Phillip Edward Island stopped being a destination and became a place I wanted to keep exploring.

A Landscape Sculpted by Time

From a distance, the islands can look almost unreal.

Phillip Edward Island is part of a vast archipelago scattered across the northern waters of Georgian Bay. From the water, the landscape can appear almost abstract: distant islands, smooth granite rising from the lake, and windswept trees silhouetted against an enormous sky.

It reminded me of a surrealist Group of Seven painting. Not because the landscape was trying to imitate art, but because it sometimes looked as though nature had already composed the painting and simply left it sitting there.

From a distance, the islands seem simple.

The closer you get, the more complicated they become.

A shoreline that looked like a single slab of granite from hundreds of metres away suddenly reveals layers of colour, fractures, textures and patterns. Smooth pink granite gives way to white quartzite and quartz, then to pegmatites whose lighter colours stand sharply against the surrounding rock.

Some of these formations are enormous and unmistakable. Others reveal themselves only to a paddler floating quietly above them, where the water becomes clear enough to expose patterns hidden beneath the surface.

The southern edge of the islands can feel almost like a coastline on an ocean. Huge expanses of smooth granite meet the open water, sometimes rising two or three stories above the lake, sometimes lying almost completely bare beneath the sky. Orange and other vividly coloured lichens cling to the exposed rock, adding another layer of colour to an already improbable palette.

The granite itself is often described simply as pink. That description is accurate, but it barely begins to explain it. Depending on the light, the rock can shift through dusty rose, salmon, muted red, grey and almost lavender tones. White quartz and quartzite cut through it, while pink pegmatites create their own dramatic patterns, sometimes looking almost as though another material has been poured into the granite and allowed to harden.

The glaciers left their signatures everywhere. Long striations run across exposed surfaces, following the direction of the ancient ice. Rounded and polished formations, chatter marks and other scars of glacial movement remain written into the rock.

The landscape is not simply old. It wears its history on the surface.

And then there are the shapes.

Some shorelines form broad, smooth curves that seem almost impossible to distinguish from the water beside them. Granite surfaces have been polished so smoothly that they resemble stone sculpted by an enormous hand. Shallow depressions collect clear water, creating natural pools and tiny granite basins that seem almost designed rather than eroded.

In places, the rock seems to imitate the lake itself. Broad glacially sculpted waves rise and fall across the shoreline, as though the motion of Georgian Bay had somehow been frozen in stone.

The farther the shoreline is explored, the more those details accumulate. A bright quartz formation here. A strange pegmatite there. A band of orange lichen. A patch of granite polished almost perfectly smooth. Beneath the kayak, another pattern appears through the water.

The water adds another dimension to all of it.

In deeper channels it becomes the familiar deep blue of Georgian Bay. Over shallow granite, it changes dramatically, revealing turquoise, emerald and translucent greens. Where unusual minerals lie beneath the surface, the colours become almost impossible to describe accurately.

Some places look less like ordinary shoreline and more like something precious that has been uncovered.

Treasures scattered across the bottom of a freshwater sea.

The vegetation changes with the landscape. Sheltered bays and narrow channels can feel surprisingly gentle, with shallow water, vegetation and softer shorelines. But on the exposed southern edges, trees become increasingly sparse and the granite dominates.

Nothing represents this environment better than the white pines.

Some grow in sheltered pockets and reach toward the sky. Others cling directly to bare granite, their trunks twisted and their branches shaped by years of wind, cold and limited soil.

They look almost like bonsai trees.

Except these were not carefully cultivated by an expert gardener. They were shaped by the landscape itself.

They may never have grown large. They grew character instead.

Their twisted trunks, asymmetrical branches and stubborn roots give each tree an individuality that is difficult to ignore. A single pine growing from an otherwise bare slab of granite can look completely out of place, yet somehow perfectly at home.

Even the air feels different. There is the familiar scent of forest and pine, but mixed with something softer and harder to identify — almost floral, carried across the warm granite and sheltered channels. It is one of those smells that I have never been able to name, but immediately recognize when I return.

And that may be what makes these islands so difficult to describe. They are not simply a collection of beautiful photographs. They are an accumulation of tiny details: rock beneath the water, lichen on a cliff, the shape of a single tree, the smell of the shoreline, the colour of the lake changing beneath the kayak.

You can look at the islands from a distance and understand why people come here.

But you have to paddle toward them to understand why they are so difficult to leave.

Three Journeys Into the Islands

There is still much more to tell.

Phillip Edward Island is not a place I have experienced in a single journey. Each trip revealed something different — sometimes because I deliberately went looking for it, and sometimes because the wind, waves and geography decided where I would go instead.

Three of those journeys will eventually become their own stories. For now, consider these the beginning of the next chapter.
